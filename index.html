How people navigate

People have found their way across oceans for generations by carefully observing the natural world. Indigenous navigators from Aotearoa, the Pacific, and beyond have used stars, weather, swell, birds, and knowledge passed down through generations to guide their journeys. Today, navigators still use a mix of old knowledge and modern tools like compasses, maps, and GPS.

Stars

Some stars act like signposts. Navigators learn where they rise, where they set, and how they move across the night sky.

Ocean

Swells, waves, wind, birds, and cloud patterns give clues about direction, distance, and land nearby.

Technology

Tools like compass, charts, and GPS can support navigation, but they work best alongside observation and knowledge.

Māori and Pacific wayfinding

Specific stars and star groups have long been used as guideposts. Māori and Pacific navigators learn the positions of stars as they rise and set, and use that pattern to keep track of direction and travel over long distances.

Across the Pacific Ocean, navigators read swell, wind, tides, and the changing tone of the sea. These clues help travellers understand where open water lies and what might be nearby.

Traditional navigation tools included practical instruments, memory aids, star maps, and knowledge shared through teaching. These supports helped skilled navigators keep direction even when technology was not available.
